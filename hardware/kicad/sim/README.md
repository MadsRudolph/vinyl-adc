# SPICE testbenches for the vinyl ADC

One KiCad sheet per block of the board, plus one for the whole modulator
loop. Open a `.kicad_pro`, then **Inspect → Simulator** and press Run: each
sheet carries its own analysis command, both as text on the sheet and in its
`.wbk` workbook.

| sheet | what it answers |
|---|---|
| `sim_a_pump` | does the charge pump make −5 V, how deep, and how clean? |
| `sim_b_reference` | is the ±2.5 V reference really ratiometric to the rail? |
| `sim_c_clock` | four exact binary clocks, and what the ripple delay costs |
| `sim_d_integrator` | is a1 what the design says, and where does the TL074 stop? |
| `sim_e_quantiser` | LM311 on one supply: threshold, common mode, and k0 |
| `sim_f_dac` | is the 1-bit feedback really ±2.5 V about the reference? |
| `sim_g_interface` | 32 L + 32 R per I2S frame, and how much setup margin is left |
| `sim_h_loop` | the whole channel — **does it survive a vinyl click?** |

Everything is generated by `tools/sim_layout.py`; edit that rather than the
sheets. It **imports the board's own layout script** and calls the same block
functions with the bench's sheet, so the charge pump, the reference, the
divider, every summing stage and the quantiser are the same drawing here as
on `../vinyl_adc.kicad_sch`. A block wired one way on the board and another
way in its testbench proves nothing, and calling one function from both is
the only way to be sure they agree.

## What SPICE is for here, and what it is not

The Python model in `../../../sim` already predicts 68 dB SNR at −8 dBFS and
does it properly, with long runs and an FFT. **These sheets do not try to
reproduce that.** 68 dB over a 20 kHz band at OSR 32 needs on the order of a
second of transient at nanosecond steps; it would run for hours and say what
`sim/verify.py` computes in minutes.

SPICE's job is the analog reality the Python model idealises: op-amp
bandwidth and saturation, the comparator's 200 ns, the charge pump under real
load, the DAC gate's output resistance, and the skew a ripple counter adds.

## Models

`models/vinyl_adc_sim.lib` holds behavioural subcircuits for every active
part, because none of them has a free redistributable vendor model. They use
only primitives every ngspice understands — B/E/G sources, switches and
terminated lossless T-lines for delay — deliberately avoiding XSPICE digital
primitives, which are not compiled into every ngspice build.

Each model's header says what it does **not** cover. `tools/test_models.py`
checks all of them against their datasheet numbers, separately from
`tools/run_sims.py`, which checks the *design* against `docs/design-notes.md`
— a model and a design check that shared an assumption would agree with each
other and both be wrong.

Three model bugs found while writing them, all worth knowing:

- **A symmetric latch sits at half supply forever.** Half-rail is an exact
  self-consistent solution, SPICE finds it, and every counter output freezes
  at 2.5 V. The keeper is centred a hair off half to break it.
- **A transparent latch must be *driven* by the one before it.** Switching
  straight from master node to slave node connects two 1 pF capacitors and
  they share charge: a 1 and a 0 average to a half, and the keeper
  regenerates that to whichever rail it happens to favour. The stage looks
  like it works and its output is noise.
- **An op-amp macromodel winds up when it saturates.** The gain node sits at
  Avol times the input error — tens of kV — and unwinding it at the slew rate
  takes milliseconds. On this board the integrators saturate on every click,
  so the model would have invented a latch-up the real part does not have.
  There is a clamp on the gain node now.

## What the simulations found

**The charge pump was wired backwards.** Both Schottkys were drawn with their
anodes and cathodes swapped, which makes the circuit a voltage doubler: the
net called `-5V` measured **+3.4 V**, which would have put +3.4 V on the V−
pin of every op-amp on the board. Nothing else caught it — `sch_score`, ERC,
the netlist read-back and `check_intent.py` all passed on that version,
because a diode wired the wrong way round is still a connected diode. The
cause was a comment that said `Device:D_Schottky` numbers its pins anode
first; it numbers them **cathode first**. Fixed, and `sim_a_pump` now checks
the pump node's polarity explicitly.

**The pump is weaker than the design assumed**: −3.87 V at 29.8 mA, not
−4.1 V at 32 mA. Its output resistance is about 16 Ω and it is set by the
74HC244's own on-resistance, not by 1/fC — 10 µF through 4 Ω is a 40 µs time
constant against a 2.6 µs half period, so the flying cap never finishes
charging. A bigger C4 or a faster PUMP will not help; only more paralleled
buffers would, and there are none left. The rail also takes **about 40 ms**
to come up, which is worth knowing before deciding a board is dead at
power-on. Ripple after the RC is **0.24 µV**, so the design's "under 1 µV"
holds.

**That shortfall costs the TL074's input common-mode margin.** The TL07x is
specified at ±11 V minimum on ±15 V rails — V− + 4 V guaranteed, V− + 3 V
typical. On a −3.87 V rail the *guaranteed* floor is **+0.13 V**, and every
virtual earth on this board sits at 0 V. It is inside the typical range
(−0.87 V) and the simulated summing nodes never go below −0.27 V, so it
works — but the 1 V of margin the design assumed from a nominal −5 V rail is
not there, and a part at the guaranteed limit would phase-invert. This is the
one finding worth acting on: anything that lightens the negative rail's load
buys margin directly.

**The loop survives the click.** 40 µs at three times full scale at
t = 1.5 ms, and by 2.2 ms the integrators are back to their normal 1.3–1.5 V
swing with the output still toggling at 50 % density. That is the single most
valuable thing on these sheets: a 1-bit third-order loop that overloads does
not recover on its own, and there are no clamp parts anywhere on this board.

**The saturation that rescues it is asymmetric**, because the rails are:
+3.19/−2.35 V at integrator 1, +3.43/−2.33 V at 2, +3.35/−2.32 V at 3. The
design assumed a symmetric ±2.7 V. It recovers anyway, and a sweep of the
Python model shows recovery is insensitive to the clamp level over at least
2.0–4.5 V, so the "1.3–1.8 × Vref window" in `design-notes` 4a is much less
knife-edge than it reads.

**Every coefficient is about 3.5 % low, and that is the TL074's 3 MHz.** The
settled integrator ramp is 96 % of 2.5 V / (Rd × C) — an ideal amplifier
gives 100.0 % and a 30 MHz one 99.7 %, so it is the part, not the
measurement. It is a systematic 3.5 % on every ai, well inside the ±10 %
Monte Carlo of `design-notes` 7, and `sim/verify.py`'s GBW sweep already
shows the loop does not care. The triangle's peak-to-peak reads 6 % low for
the same reason: the corners are rounded, but no charge is lost.

**k0 measures 0.228**, against the design's −0.225 and the E96 pair's
−0.2278, read straight off the threshold shift as the DAC switches. The
LM311's propagation delay comes out at 195 ns against the 200 ns budgeted.

**The I2S setup margin is 145 ns, not 163 ns.** The ripple counter puts
31 ns of skew between BCLK and LRCLK, and the mux and level shifters take
their share; what survives is still more than four times what the Pi needs.
64 BCLK per LRCLK frame and a clean 1.536 MHz DIN confirm the two channels
land in alternate bit slots.

**The DAC's two levels are not quite symmetric**: 0.09 % at integrator 1 and
1.3 % at integrator 2, from the 74HC04's own 50 Ω against 14k7 and 13k0.
That is a gain error and a DC offset in the feedback, never distortion — but
it is the reason integrator 2's offset leg would be better as 13k0 + 50 Ω if
anyone ever cares.

## Traps this work hit, for the next person

**ngspice cannot read the board's component values.** Every value on this
board is written the IEC 60062 way — `14k7`, `4R75`, `1M0` — which is correct
for a BOM and unreadable to SPICE. ngspice takes the leading number, looks
for a scale suffix and throws the rest away:

| on the sheet | ngspice reads | intended |
|---|---|---|
| `14k7` | 14 kΩ | 14.7 kΩ |
| `5k90` | 5 kΩ | 5.9 kΩ |
| `4R75` | 4 Ω | 4.75 Ω |
| `1M0` | **1 mΩ** | 1 MΩ |

Nothing warns. The deck exports, the sim runs, and it is a different circuit
— the `1M0` terminations were dead shorts, which is why the first clock bench
produced no clock at all. `sim_layout.py` now gives every passive a
`Sim.Params` override carrying the same value spelled the way ngspice reads
it, while the Value field keeps the notation the board and the BOM use.

**KiCad's SPICE exporter has no notion of symbol units.** A multi-unit part
exports as one broken device per reference — a TL074 with all five units
placed comes out as `U1 __U1` and nothing else. Every gate a bench simulates
therefore needs a single-unit symbol carrying its own supply pins, which is
what `lib/vinyl_adc_sim.kicad_sym` is for.

**Two power symbols can land on each other.** Two D flip-flops 35.56 mm apart
put the upper one's GND symbol exactly on the lower one's +5V symbol — a dead
short that `sh.check()` does not see, because it does not compare power
symbols, and that ERC reports as the confusing "+5V and GND are attached to
the same items". Rows of parts that carry their own rail symbols need more
pitch than their pins suggest.

**A plain wire crossing is safe here; a T is not.** `schdraw` seeds its
junction search from wire *endpoints* and pin positions, so two wires that
merely cross get no dot and stay separate. A wire whose end lands on another
wire's interior does get one. Worth knowing before rewriting a route to avoid
a crossing that was never a problem.

## The links from the board sheet

`../vinyl_adc.kicad_sch` carries a clickable index of these benches, and each
bench carries a link back. They are hyperlinks, not hierarchical sheets — see
the comment above `SIM_LINKS` in `../tools/vinyl_adc_layout.py` for what a
hierarchy would have done to the board's netlist.

Two things decide whether a click works:

- **The href needs a scheme.** A bare relative path or a bare `${KIPRJMOD}/…`
  is rejected when the file loads, so Eeschema refuses to open the schematic
  at all — not a broken link, a broken file. `file:sim/<name>.kicad_sch` is
  what is used here.
- **The `.kicad_sch` file association must point at the KiCad you run.** The
  links name the sheet rather than the project, because Windows opens a
  `.kicad_pro` with the project manager and leaves you one click short. If a
  click reports *"Documentation file '…' not found"* while the file is
  plainly there, the handler is stale, not the link.
